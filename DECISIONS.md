# Decision log

Every non-obvious choice in this project, why it was made, and what breaks if it
was the wrong call. Numbers cited here are regenerated by
`secom run` (or `scripts/run_analysis.py`) into `reports/results.json`.

The dataset is UCI SECOM: 1,567 production runs, 590 unnamed sensor signals,
pass/fail from in-house line test, 19 Jul – 17 Oct 2008 (89 days), 104 failures
(6.64%).

---

## Data handling

### D1. Sensors are identified by 1-based raw column position (`S001`…`S590`)

The source files have no header and no data dictionary. Any name I invented
would imply physical knowledge I do not have. Position-based IDs mean every
claim in this project can be traced back to a specific column of the published
file. `io.load_raw` hard-errors if the matrix is not 1567×590, because a silent
shape change would invalidate every identifier.

**If wrong:** nothing — this is a labelling convention, not an inference.

### D2. Sparse sensors dropped above 20% missing

Missingness is bimodal, not continuous: 506 sensors sit at or below 3.3%, then
there is a gap to the next group starting at 16.6%. The largest gap anywhere in
the distribution is between 17.4% and 45.6%.

**Any threshold in [0.175, 0.456] drops exactly the same 32 sensors.** The
choice is insensitive across a factor-of-2.6 band, so it is not a tuning knob.
20% is stated because it is a round number inside that band.

**If wrong:** a threshold below 0.033 would drop 506 sensors and gut the
analysis; the gap structure is what rules that out.

### D3. Constants are resolved before duplicates

116 sensors have a single distinct value. 111 of them are all-zero and collapse
into 7 groups of byte-identical columns — one group has 41 members.

A frozen column is trivially a duplicate of every other frozen column. Reporting
them as duplicates would hide the more specific and more actionable defect (a
dead sensor). Ordering constants first means the disposition counts are a true
partition of the 590.

**Consequence for anyone reading other SECOM write-ups:** the 104 "duplicate
columns" are a *subset* of the 116 constants, not an additional 104. After
removing constants, **no two live sensors are exact duplicates.** An analysis
that reports both figures additively is double-counting.

### D4. Near-duplicates removed at |r| ≥ 0.999

30 surviving sensors are collinear with an earlier survivor to three nines. They
are removed because they inflate the apparent dimensionality — and therefore the
multiple-comparison burden — without adding information.

0.999 rather than 0.95: at 0.95 the cut starts removing sensors that are merely
correlated because they measure related process states, which is a modelling
judgement, not a data defect. This threshold only removes near-algebraic
redundancy.

**If wrong:** a looser threshold discards real signal; a tighter one leaves
redundancy that makes the FDR correction unnecessarily harsh. Checked by
re-running at 0.99 and 0.9999 — retention moves 412 → 405 / 418, and no
conclusion in this report changes.

### D5. Imputation is causal (trailing rolling median, window 25)

**This is the load-bearing decision of the whole project.**

Every imputed value is computed only from runs that had already happened.
A centred rolling median or a global median would impute better and would also
leak future information backwards. Since the stated purpose is to decide whether
these sensors can support *live* monitoring, an imputation that could not be
executed in real time would invalidate the answer.

Cascade: trailing rolling median (25 runs ≈ half a shift at the observed
cadence) → expanding median over all prior runs → first observed value.

Only the last step looks forward, it is unavoidable (there is no prior
information for a sensor missing on run 1), and its exposure is measured:
**20 cells out of 645,604 = 0.0031%.** Total imputation touches 1.22% of cells.

Enforced by test — `test_imputation_cannot_see_the_future` mutates every value
after a gap and asserts the imputed value is unchanged.

**If wrong:** the forward-evaluation results become optimistic and the central
comparison against the shuffled protocol collapses.

### D6. Missingness is imputed, not carried as an indicator

Tested rather than assumed. Fisher's exact test of missing-vs-present against
pass/fail, for all 538 sensors with any gaps: **0 significant after FDR**
(minimum q = 0.102). Only 8 sensors reach raw p < 0.05, against ~27 expected by
chance — fewer than chance, not more.

Missingness here is uninformative, so imputing it destroys nothing.

**If wrong:** had any sensor shown informative missingness, imputation would
have erased real signal and the indicator would have been mandatory.

## Monitoring design

### D7. Phase I baseline is ISO weeks 35–39

717 runs, 22 failures, p₀ = 3.07%. Chosen as the longest contiguous stretch
whose weekly standardised proportions all fall within ±2.4σ of their own mean.

Control limits are estimated once here and held fixed. Recomputing limits from
all data — the default in most tutorials — lets an excursion inflate the very
limits meant to catch it.

**If wrong:** a baseline that secretly contains an excursion produces limits too
wide to detect anything. Guarded by the self-check in D12: the baseline window
produces 2 alarm episodes against 1.4 expected, which is consistent with a
window that is genuinely stable.

### D8. Weekly subgroups for the proportion chart, and a stated blind spot

Daily subgroups give a median n of 15 — far too small. Weekly gives 14 subgroups
at median n = 120. 14 is below the ~25 subgroups Phase I normally wants, and
that limitation is real.

More importantly: **at p̄ = 6.64% a subgroup needs n ≥ 9(1−p)/p = 127 runs
before the lower limit clears zero.** 7 of 14 weeks fall below that. The chart
is therefore structurally incapable of signalling that yield has *improved* —
which is exactly what happened here. This is surfaced in the model as
`[Improvement Detectable]` rather than left for a user to discover.

### D9. Overdispersion is tested, and the correction is applied

σ_z = 1.463 — the week-to-week variation is 46% wider than binomial sampling can
explain (χ² = 60.0 on 13 df, p = 5.2×10⁻⁸). The Laney p′ correction is therefore
warranted.

Consequence, reported rather than buried: the naive chart flags **1** week
(W31); the corrected chart flags **0**. Widening the limits by 1.46× absorbs
every point.

The correct reading is *not* "the process is fine". It is that the weekly failure
rate varies far more than sampling explains, so a proportion chart at this grain
cannot separate signal from process noise, and the variation has to be attributed
another way.

**If wrong:** applying Laney when the data is genuinely binomial makes the chart
insensitive. Guarded by testing dispersion explicitly instead of assuming it, in
both directions (`test_sigma_z_is_about_one_for_binomial_data`).

### D10. Two centre lines are exported, deliberately

- `ucl` / `lcl` — centred on all 14 weeks (p̄ = 0.0664). Answers the
  retrospective question *"was this process ever in control?"*
- `ucl_phase1` / `lcl_phase1` — centred on Phase I (p̄ = 0.0307). Answers the
  monitoring question *"is this week consistent with baseline?"*

Same data, two different questions. The Power BI measures reproduce the
Phase I-centred version; agreement was verified to 8×10⁻¹⁷.

Within `p_chart`, the centre line and the dispersion estimate are separately
controllable. The centre must be pinned to a stable window; the dispersion
should use every subgroup available, because it is a variance component, the
moving-range estimator is insensitive to level shifts by construction, and
Phase I here is only five subgroups — far too thin to estimate a dispersion
factor from.

### D11. A g-chart was added, and its limitation is stated

To cover D8's blind spot, a runs-between-failures chart with exact geometric
limits. It needs no subgrouping and its upper limit responds to a single long
clean run.

**Stated limitation:** P(gap = 0) equals p exactly, so once p exceeds α/2 —
about 0.14% — the lower quantile pins to zero and no single gap, however short,
can breach it. At the 3.07% baseline this chart detects **improvement only**.
Asserted in `test_lower_limit_is_inactive_at_realistic_failure_rates` so it
cannot be quietly forgotten, and the result object emits a
`LOWER LIMIT INACTIVE` note at runtime.

This is why the g-chart is not the answer on its own.

### D12. Bernoulli CUSUM for degradation, threshold set by simulation

Shewhart charts judge each subgroup alone, so they react to spikes and are worst
at exactly what happened here — a sustained level shift. The CUSUM accumulates
the per-run log-likelihood ratio and needs no subgrouping at all.

Design: p₀ = 0.0307 (Phase I) → p₁ = 0.0614 (a doubling, as the smallest shift
worth detecting). Threshold h = 1.822 obtained by **simulation** to give an
in-control ARL of ~500 runs (achieved 509), not guessed. Simulation rather than
a Markov approximation because the Bernoulli CUSUM lives on a lattice of two
increment sizes, where continuous ARL approximations are poor at low p₀.

### D13. The CUSUM resets on signal

Found by inspecting the figure, not by reasoning: without a reset the opening
excursion drives the statistic to ~28, and since each passing run pays down only
log((1−p₁)/(1−p₀)) ≈ 0.031, it would take roughly 900 consecutive good runs to
fall back below the limit. The chart read as alarming for the entire 89 days,
including the five weeks the process was demonstrably healthy.

Reset-on-signal is how a CUSUM is actually operated: it signals, an engineer
investigates, the chart restarts against the process as it now stands. Covered
by `test_reset_prevents_a_permanent_alarm`.

## Inference

### D14. FDR control plus an effect size, always

590 sensors means 590 simultaneous tests; at α = 0.05 roughly 30 false positives
are expected under a global null. Benjamini-Hochberg q-values throughout
(validated against `statsmodels` to 2×10⁻¹⁶).

But significance alone is not enough in the other direction either: with n = 449
vs 717, **244 of 412 sensors (59%) shift significantly between regimes.** Only 8
have |Cliff's δ| > 0.5. A q-value here measures sample size as much as it
measures effect.

Rankings use the rank-based test and Cliff's δ rather than Welch and Cohen's d.
Several SECOM sensors have excess kurtosis above 60; for a number of them
Cohen's d reads ~0.15 while Cliff's δ reads ~0.65, meaning the distributions
separate cleanly but a mean-based measure is swamped by the tails.

### D15. A root-cause claim must survive the time confound

**71% of retained sensors show significant monotone drift against run order.**
The failure rate also changes over time. So any sensor that drifts will correlate
with failure for reasons that have nothing to do with causing it.

The gate applied: a sensor must discriminate fail from pass *within* the stable
Phase I window, where the regime is constant.

Effect on the candidate list:

| Screen | Sensors passing |
|---|---|
| Shift with yield regime (q<0.05) | 244 |
| Associate with failure, all runs (q<0.05) | 17 |
| **Also discriminate within stable regime** | **1** |

16 of the 17 whole-dataset hits have substantial drift and a non-significant
within-regime p-value. They are confounded, and reporting them as root causes
would be wrong. Only **S060** survives.

**If wrong:** without this gate the report would have named 17 root causes, 16
of them artefacts.

### D16. Forward chronological evaluation is primary; shuffled is a contrast

The published SECOM baselines use 10-fold CV, which trains on October to predict
August. Given 71% drift and a failure rate that halves partway through, a
shuffled split lets a model learn *when* a run happened.

A monitoring model can only see the past, so the forward split is the only
protocol that answers the deployment question. The shuffled result is computed
anyway, to measure how much optimism the shuffle buys.

Robustness: the forward result is not one lucky cut. Rolling-origin evaluation
across 8 cut points gives mean AUC 0.641 (sd 0.106). A grouped-by-week CV lands
at 0.652, between the two — showing the leakage is partly within-week and partly
future-information.

### D17. Class imbalance handled by weighting, not resampling

`class_weight='balanced'` rather than SMOTE or over/under-sampling, so no
synthetic rows enter the evaluation. With 104 positives, synthetic minority
points interpolated between neighbours would be difficult to distinguish from
leakage in a time-ordered setting.

### D18. Two model families, deliberately different

Regularised logistic regression is the reference — linear, inspectable, and the
closest match to the linear classifiers behind the published baselines.
Gradient boosting is there to test whether the ceiling is set by the model or by
the data. It is not better (forward AUC 0.565 vs 0.606), so the limit is
informational.

## Delivery

### D19. `fact_measurement` carries 35 watchlist sensors, not all 412

A full unpivot is 645,604 rows. Power BI handles that, but it refreshes slowly
and nobody scrolls 412 sensors. The watchlist — every sensor with tier-1 or
tier-2 evidence plus the top regime movers — gives 54,845 rows and matches how a
sensor chart is actually used. The full cleaned table is exported separately
(`data/processed/secom_clean_wide.csv`) for anyone who wants to widen it.

`dim_sensor` deliberately keeps **all 590**, including the 178 excluded ones, so
"why is this sensor not on the chart?" is answerable inside the report.

### D20. Where each calculation lives

| Calculation | Where | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Control limits | **DAX** | Must respond to slicers, or a chart filtered to one shift shows limits from all shifts |
| Centre line / baseline | **DAX, with `REMOVEFILTERS`** | Must *not* respond to slicers, or an excursion redefines normal as you drill into it |
| Robust z, Phase I limits per sensor | **Python, precomputed** | Depend on Phase I median/MAD; recomputing under filter context would silently move the baseline |
| CUSUM alarm *episodes* | **Python, precomputed flag** | Needs a row-ordered lag, which DAX does awkwardly and slowly |
| Triage capture rate | **DAX** | Deliberately interactive — the reviewer drags the budget slider and sees the weak lift themselves |

### D21. Timestamps exported as ISO-8601, all Power Query types declared explicitly

The raw SECOM timestamps are day-first (`dd/MM/yyyy`). Power BI applies file
locale on import and will silently read 07/10/2008 as 10 July. Separately,
`model_score` is null outside the forward test window, and Power Query's
type inference samples the first 200 rows, sees only nulls, and types the column
as text — breaking every downstream aggregation.

## Scope boundaries

### D22. No capability analysis (Cp / Cpk / Ppk)

Capability requires specification limits. SECOM publishes none, and the sensors
are unnamed and unitless, so no engineering limit can be inferred. Computing
capability against control limits instead of spec limits is a common error that
produces an authoritative-looking number with no meaning. Omitted deliberately.

### D23. No physical interpretation of any sensor

The signals are anonymised. S060 is described by its statistical behaviour only.
Naming it as a specific process parameter would be invention.

### D24. What this analysis cannot establish

- **Causation.** S060 is an association that survives a time-confound check.
  Confirming it needs a designed experiment, not more of this data.
- **Whether the W35 improvement was a process change or a measurement change.**
  A change in test limits or gauge would look identical in this data.
- **Generalisation beyond 2008 and beyond this line.** 89 days, one product.
- **S060's stability.** Its association is strong in W29–34 (δ = 0.21) and
  W35–39 (δ = 0.57) but **absent in W40–42** (δ = −0.04, p = 0.76). Either the
  mechanism changed or the association was never causal. Stated in the report as
  a caveat on the recommendation, not omitted.

## Engineering

### D25. A staged pipeline driven by one config file

The analysis started as one ~600-line script with its constants hard-coded at
the top and in the middle of expressions. It is now nine stages in
`src/secom/pipeline/stages/`, each declaring the artifacts it requires and
produces, and every constant lives in `config/default.yaml`.

Three reasons, in order of importance:

- **The insensitivity claims become checkable.** D2 and D4 say the results do
  not depend on the exact thresholds. With the thresholds in a config file,
  anyone can test that with `secom run --config`, instead of editing code and
  trusting that they changed every occurrence.
- **Extensions cannot disturb the core.** A new analysis is a new stage that
  reads existing artifacts. The core stages, and therefore every committed
  number, stay untouched. A registry test enforces that every requirement is
  produced upstream and that `figures` and `export` stay last.
- **Partial runs.** `--only` runs a stage and its dependencies, so iterating on
  the CUSUM does not repeat the model evaluation.

Config validation is strict: an unknown key or a wrong type is an error. A
lenient loader would let a misspelt threshold fall back to its default without
anyone noticing, which is exactly the kind of quiet change the rest of this log
exists to prevent.

The refactor was accepted only because it changed no output: every CSV and
`results.json` reproduces byte-for-byte, and the figures match the previous
rendering.

**If wrong:** the cost is indirection. A reader now follows the analysis across
a dozen short modules instead of reading one script top to bottom. The stage
docstrings and `secom stages -v` are there to keep that cheap.

## Multivariate monitoring

### D26. PCA model: Phase I autoscaling, and only retained components are inverted

The PCA model is fitted on the Phase I baseline only (717 runs) and held fixed.
Sensors are scaled by their baseline mean and SD, not by median / MAD. The T²
and SPE limit theory is built on the sample mean and covariance, and 8 sensors
have a baseline MAD of zero, so a robust z is undefined for them. S075 is
constant within the baseline and is left out, leaving 411 sensors.

The baseline matrix has full rank (411 < 717), but its smallest eigenvalue is
3.5×10⁻¹¹. T² is therefore computed from the retained components only (condition
number 18.9), never from the inverse of the full covariance.

**If wrong:** a T² built on the full inverse is dominated by directions with
near-zero variance, so floating-point noise would decide which runs alarm.

### D27. Limits from out-of-fold baseline quantiles, at α = 0.01

The F / Beta limits for T² and the Jackson–Mudholkar limit for SPE assume
multivariate normality. On this data they put **15.8% of baseline runs** over
the limit, against a nominal 2% or less. Under those limits the chart flags 86%
of the degraded period and 83% of Phase II, and the flags have no relationship
with failure (p = 0.84).

Each baseline run is instead scored by a model fitted on the other four
contiguous time blocks. Each limit is set at the (1 − α) quantile of those
out-of-fold scores. In-sample scores would understate SPE, because the
components were fitted to those very rows.

α is 0.01 per chart, not the 3-sigma 0.0027 used for the univariate charts. The
out-of-fold statistics have excess kurtosis of about 712. Their 99.73% quantile
rests on about two of 717 runs, so it can't be estimated (it lands at T² = 8,328,
which is set by a few gross outliers).

**If wrong:** with the theoretical limits, the chart's apparent sensitivity is
pure miscalibration. A smaller α is not usable until there is a longer baseline
to estimate the tail from. Both limit schemes are kept in `results.json` so the
contrast stays visible.

### D28. Number of components: 80% of baseline variance, with a sensitivity sweep

A = 81 components, the smallest number explaining at least 80% of baseline
variance. The spectrum is flat (the first component explains 6.7%, and 105
eigenvalues exceed 1), so no elbow rule applies. The analysis is re-run at
50–95% (A = 30 to 150):

- The out-of-fold baseline alarm rate stays at 1.1–1.3%.
- The theoretical baseline rate stays at 13.4–16.0%.
- The degraded-period failure association stays at p = 0.001–0.012.
- Phase II never shows an association (p = 0.62 throughout).

**If wrong:** nothing in the verdict depends on A within that range. A outside
it was not tested.

### D29. The PCA chart is not adopted as the yield monitor

With calibrated limits it alarms on 6.9% of runs in the period when yield was
worst. The alarms are mostly single-sensor outliers: the top 15 contributors
have a median excess kurtosis of 276, and in 38% of alarmed runs one sensor is
more than half the SPE. A sensor's contribution is unrelated to its drift
(ρ = −0.02). The alarms mark failures only in the degraded period (p = 0.011),
which does not survive correcting for the three windows tested and shifts with
α (p = 0.092 at α = 0.05, 0.50 at α = 0.005).

The run-to-first-signal is not compared with the CUSUM as a race. The PCA
chart's 1.1% per-run false-alarm rate implies an in-control ARL of about 90
runs, against the CUSUM's 509, so its earlier first signal (run 4 vs 12)
reflects a chart that alarms more often.

S060 ranks 259th of 411 in SPE contribution, so contribution plots do not
rediscover the one sensor that survived the confound gate.

**If wrong:** if the degraded-period association is real, the chart is a useful
excursion flag in a degraded process. That is a reason to keep it as a
secondary flag on the heavy-tailed sensors, with out-of-fold limits, and
re-test it against new data. It is not a reason to replace the CUSUM.

## Detection power and change points

### D30. Detection power is simulated on the real chart designs and checked against exact values

`shift_simulation` runs every scheme over the same simulated pass/fail streams,
with the Phase I rate, weekly subgroup sizes, Laney σ_z, CUSUM thresholds and
g-chart limits taken from the pipeline's own artifacts. Nothing is re-derived
for the simulation.

The conventions are stated and fixed:
- **Zero-state.** The shift is present from run 1.
- **Subgroups.** Weekly subgroups cycle through the 14 observed weekly sizes,
  starting at a random week for each stream.
- **g-chart.** It runs live, signalling as soon as the count of passes exceeds
  the UCL instead of waiting for the next failure. This is the same decision
  rule, made earlier.
- **Common random numbers.** One uniform matrix is shared across shift ratios.
  This makes the detection probability of the one-sided CUSUM exactly monotone
  in the shift, and a test asserts that.

Every Shewhart detection probability also has an exact binomial value, and the
stage reports the largest simulated-vs-exact gap (0.0146 against a worst-case
MC standard error of 0.0079).

**If wrong:** if the simulation used idealised designs instead of the deployed
ones, it would measure a different system from the one recommended. Zero-state
timing flatters the weekly charts (their first subgroup is wholly post-shift),
so if that convention is wrong, the CUSUM's advantage is understated, not
overstated.

### D31. CUSUMs are also compared at matched false-alarm probability

As designed (ARL₀ ≈ 500 runs), the upper CUSUM false-alarms in 96.6% of
1,567-run campaigns, against 7.3% for the weekly p-chart. Comparing them as
deployed would partly compare false-alarm budgets.

So each one-sided CUSUM is also run with h set so that P(false alarm within the
campaign) equals that of the chart it is compared with:
- the upper CUSUM is matched to the p-chart, using the exact binomial value;
- the lower CUSUM is matched to the g-chart, using a simulated value from
  independent streams.

A CUSUM signals within the horizon exactly when its path maximum exceeds h, so
h is a quantile of simulated in-control path maxima. There is no search, and no
reuse of the evaluation streams.

**If wrong:** without matching, the report would credit the CUSUM with 99% vs
50% detection at a doubling. Matched, it is 70% vs 50%. The conclusion survives
the matching, but the size of the advantage does not.

### D32. A detection must point in the direction of the shift

A detection counts only signals in the direction of the shift:
- **After a fall in the fail rate:** a p-chart point above the UCL, or an upper
  CUSUM alarm, is a false alarm, not a detection.
- **In control (ratio 1):** every signal counts, as a false alarm.

**If wrong:** counting any signal as a detection credits two-sided charts with
improvement "detections" that are really upper-limit false alarms. That would
have reported a non-zero improvement-detection rate for a p-chart whose lower
limit is zero in every week, which is the exact blind spot this analysis exists
to measure.

### D33. The change point is the exact MLE; further splits need a day-block permutation p < α

The single change point is found by profile likelihood over every split, with
a minimum segment of 30 runs. This is exact, so no PELT or other search
approximation is needed at n = 1,567.

Significance is assessed by permutation, two ways:
- **Run-level:** shuffles individual runs, which assumes runs are exchangeable.
- **Day-block:** shuffles whole calendar days, which keeps within-day clustering
  intact.

The weekly rates are overdispersed (D9), so run-level exchangeability is
optimistic: the day-block null's 95th percentile of the maximum LR is 18.07,
against 10.42 for the run-level null. Binary segmentation therefore splits only
while the day-block p-value is below α = 0.05.

The location interval is a basic parametric bootstrap from the fitted
two-rate model.

**If wrong:**
- **Run-level stopping rule:** binary segmentation would be more willing to
  accept spurious secondary changes driven by bad days.
- **Bootstrap interval:** it ignores the same clustering, so it is narrower
  than the truth. It is reported as an interval for the location, not as
  evidence about how many changes there are.

## Replay monitor

### D34. The monitor reads committed outputs and runs on the standard library

`secom monitor` loads its design from `reports/results.json` and
`data/powerbi/` instead of rerunning the pipeline. It starts in about a second,
and it shows exactly the design the report quotes: p₀, p₁ and h come from the
same `results.json` the write-up cites.

The server is `http.server` and the page loads one pinned chart library
(Chart.js 4.4.1) from a CDN. A Streamlit or Dash app would mean a heavy
dependency tree for a single page, and on locked-down machines (this project
already hits a blocked matplotlib DLL) every compiled extension is another
thing that may refuse to load.

Correctness is pinned by test, not by eye. The online CUSUM reproduces
`spc.bernoulli_cusum` bit for bit on random streams, with and without reset,
and matches the exported `fact_run.cusum` to the CSV's 4×10⁻¹⁶ rounding.

**If wrong:** the monitor goes stale if someone changes the config and does not
rerun `secom run`. The loader names the files it read, and the page displays the
design it is running.

### D35. The statistic resets on signal; the alarm does not

D13 resets the CUSUM the moment it signals, which is right for the statistic.
An engineer is still investigating, though, so the monitor separates the two:

- The **statistic** resets and keeps watching.
- The **alarm** stays open until acknowledged, with an optional note.
- **Repeat signals** while an alarm is open count against that alarm instead of
  raising new ones.

On this campaign the difference is large. The CUSUM signals on 21 runs. Left
unacknowledged, they are one alarm raised at run 12. Raising 21 alarms for one
degraded period is how a line learns to ignore its monitor.

**If wrong:** an acknowledged-too-early alarm could hide a second, unrelated
excursion behind the first. Acknowledgement closes the alarm, so the next signal
opens a fresh one; nothing is suppressed after an acknowledgement.

### D36. The monitor's g-chart uses the Phase I rate; S060 never alarms

The exported `fact_gchart` uses the all-data rate (p = 6.64%, UCL 96). That
chart answers the retrospective question. A monitor must be designed on the
baseline it protects, so the monitor's g-chart uses p₀ = 3.07% (UCL 212), the
same Phase I design as the CUSUM. It signals improvement as soon as the open gap
crosses the UCL, without waiting for the next failure. Over this campaign it
never signals: the longest gap is 99 runs. The page says so and states the
inactive lower limit (D11).

S060 is shown against its Phase I individuals limits, but it is informational
only. 278 of 1,567 readings (17.7%) are outside limits that expect 0.27%,
because the sensor drifts. An alarm would fire continually. The panel reports
recent exceedances against chance instead, which is what a watchlist is for.

**If wrong:** if S060 is causal after all, the designed experiment D24 calls for
is the way to promote it to an alarm, with limits set on a stable process. That
is not something to decide from this panel.

## Validation

### D37. Validate on Tennessee Eastman, as a separate command

SECOM has no fault labels, so no analysis of SECOM alone can show that a chart
detects a real fault. The Tennessee Eastman benchmark has 21 faults injected
at known samples and a published PCA result on the same files. Running the
repo's own `secom.mspc` and chart code on it, unchanged, tests the
implementation where the answer is known.

The validation is `secom validate tep`, not a pipeline stage, so the SECOM
analysis never depends on a second dataset. The TEP files are fetched into
`data/raw/tep/` with their SHA-256 digests recorded, and they are never
committed. Only the derived `reports/validation/tep_results.json` is.

**If wrong:** if TEP were a poor proxy, a pass would prove little. That is why
the claim is limited to implementation correctness and the cause of the
calibration failure (report §9, "What this validation does not establish"),
not SECOM detection power.

### D38. Benchmark settings: A = 11, α = 0.01, textbook limits primary

A = 11 retained components and α = 0.01 per chart are the settings commonly
attributed to the Chiang, Russell & Braatz (2001) PCA benchmark on these
files. The textbook F and Jackson–Mudholkar limits are primary because they
are what that benchmark uses. The benchmark detection rates are a
transcription from secondary reproductions, not checked against the book, so
the tests assert only qualitative facts (easy faults caught, faults 3/9/15
not, fault 4 an SPE fault). They do not assert the table.

The cross-validated limits from the SECOM multivariate stage are reported
alongside but are not primary here. On TEP they improve SPE (7.1% → 2.2%
false alarms on `d00_te`) but worsen T² (1.7% → 4.7%), so neither scheme
dominates.

**If wrong:** if the benchmark used another A, the detection comparison
shifts. The sensitivity table (A = 5 to 24) shows mean detection over the
detectable faults moving only 0.61–0.65 (T²) and 0.76–0.84 (SPE), and the
qualitative facts hold throughout.

### D39. TEP univariate charts use the overall SD, not the moving range

The family of 52 per-variable charts with moving-range sigma alarms on 100%
of the independent normal run. The median lag-1 autocorrelation is 0.55, the
moving range then underestimates sigma (median 0.40 of the SD), and the
shortfall tracks the autocorrelation (Spearman ρ = −0.94). With the overall
training SD, the same Šidák family alarms on 4.0%.

Šidák rather than Bonferroni because it is exact for independent charts and
still conservative under the positive dependence these variables have. An
FDR rule doesn't fit, because an alarm is a per-sample decision over the
whole family, and the family-wise rate is the quantity an operator lives
with.

**If wrong:** the SD estimate is inflated by any slow drift within the
training run, which widens the limits. On this training file that cost is
small: univariate detection matches SPE on most faults.

### D40. The moving-range finding is recorded for SECOM, not acted on here

SECOM baseline sensors are close to independent (median lag-1
autocorrelation 0.04), so the TEP failure mode does not apply. Their
moving-range sigma is still a median 0.78 of the SD, because of heavy tails
and level shifts, and the per-sensor Phase I limits (D20) are correspondingly
narrower than SD-based limits would be. That is recorded, not changed: it
alters no conclusion in the report, and changing the sensor limits would
change committed outputs outside this validation's scope.

**If wrong:** a beyond-limits count on SECOM sensor charts (e.g. S060 on the
watchlist) overstates excursions relative to an SD-based chart.
`[Violation Ratio vs Chance]` in the DAX already warns against reading raw
counts.
