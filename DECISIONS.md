# Decision log

Every non-obvious choice in this project, why it was made, and what breaks if it
was the wrong call. Numbers cited here are regenerated by
`scripts/run_analysis.py` into `reports/results.json`.

The dataset is UCI SECOM: 1,567 production runs, 590 unnamed sensor signals,
pass/fail from in-house line test, 19 Jul – 17 Oct 2008 (89 days), 104 failures
(6.64%).

---

## Data handling

### D1. Sensors are identified by 1-based raw column position (`S001`…`S590`)

The source files have no header and no data dictionary. Any name I invented
would imply physical knowledge I do not have. Position-based IDs mean every
claim in this project can be traced back to a specific column of the published
file. `io.load_raw` hard-errors if the matrix is not 1567×590, because a silent
shape change would invalidate every identifier.

**If wrong:** nothing — this is a labelling convention, not an inference.

### D2. Sparse sensors dropped above 20% missing

Missingness is bimodal, not continuous: 506 sensors sit at or below 3.3%, then
there is a gap to the next group starting at 16.6%. The largest gap anywhere in
the distribution is between 17.4% and 45.6%.

**Any threshold in [0.175, 0.456] drops exactly the same 32 sensors.** The
choice is insensitive across a factor-of-2.6 band, so it is not a tuning knob.
20% is stated because it is a round number inside that band.

**If wrong:** a threshold below 0.033 would drop 506 sensors and gut the
analysis; the gap structure is what rules that out.

### D3. Constants are resolved before duplicates

116 sensors have a single distinct value. 111 of them are all-zero and collapse
into 7 groups of byte-identical columns — one group has 41 members.

A frozen column is trivially a duplicate of every other frozen column. Reporting
them as duplicates would hide the more specific and more actionable defect (a
dead sensor). Ordering constants first means the disposition counts are a true
partition of the 590.

**Consequence for anyone reading other SECOM write-ups:** the 104 "duplicate
columns" are a *subset* of the 116 constants, not an additional 104. After
removing constants, **no two live sensors are exact duplicates.** An analysis
that reports both figures additively is double-counting.

### D4. Near-duplicates removed at |r| ≥ 0.999

30 surviving sensors are collinear with an earlier survivor to three nines. They
are removed because they inflate the apparent dimensionality — and therefore the
multiple-comparison burden — without adding information.

0.999 rather than 0.95: at 0.95 the cut starts removing sensors that are merely
correlated because they measure related process states, which is a modelling
judgement, not a data defect. This threshold only removes near-algebraic
redundancy.

**If wrong:** a looser threshold discards real signal; a tighter one leaves
redundancy that makes the FDR correction unnecessarily harsh. Checked by
re-running at 0.99 and 0.9999 — retention moves 412 → 405 / 418, and no
conclusion in this report changes.

### D5. Imputation is causal (trailing rolling median, window 25)

**This is the load-bearing decision of the whole project.**

Every imputed value is computed only from runs that had already happened.
A centred rolling median or a global median would impute better and would also
leak future information backwards. Since the stated purpose is to decide whether
these sensors can support *live* monitoring, an imputation that could not be
executed in real time would invalidate the answer.

Cascade: trailing rolling median (25 runs ≈ half a shift at the observed
cadence) → expanding median over all prior runs → first observed value.

Only the last step looks forward, it is unavoidable (there is no prior
information for a sensor missing on run 1), and its exposure is measured:
**20 cells out of 645,604 = 0.0031%.** Total imputation touches 1.22% of cells.

Enforced by test — `test_imputation_cannot_see_the_future` mutates every value
after a gap and asserts the imputed value is unchanged.

**If wrong:** the forward-evaluation results become optimistic and the central
comparison against the shuffled protocol collapses.

### D6. Missingness is imputed, not carried as an indicator

Tested rather than assumed. Fisher's exact test of missing-vs-present against
pass/fail, for all 538 sensors with any gaps: **0 significant after FDR**
(minimum q = 0.102). Only 8 sensors reach raw p < 0.05, against ~27 expected by
chance — fewer than chance, not more.

Missingness here is uninformative, so imputing it destroys nothing.

**If wrong:** had any sensor shown informative missingness, imputation would
have erased real signal and the indicator would have been mandatory.

## Monitoring design

### D7. Phase I baseline is ISO weeks 35–39

717 runs, 22 failures, p₀ = 3.07%. Chosen as the longest contiguous stretch
whose weekly standardised proportions all fall within ±2.4σ of their own mean.

Control limits are estimated once here and held fixed. Recomputing limits from
all data — the default in most tutorials — lets an excursion inflate the very
limits meant to catch it.

**If wrong:** a baseline that secretly contains an excursion produces limits too
wide to detect anything. Guarded by the self-check in D12: the baseline window
produces 2 alarm episodes against 1.4 expected, which is consistent with a
window that is genuinely stable.

### D8. Weekly subgroups for the proportion chart, and a stated blind spot

Daily subgroups give a median n of 15 — far too small. Weekly gives 14 subgroups
at median n = 120. 14 is below the ~25 subgroups Phase I normally wants, and
that limitation is real.

More importantly: **at p̄ = 6.64% a subgroup needs n ≥ 9(1−p)/p = 127 runs
before the lower limit clears zero.** 7 of 14 weeks fall below that. The chart
is therefore structurally incapable of signalling that yield has *improved* —
which is exactly what happened here. This is surfaced in the model as
`[Improvement Detectable]` rather than left for a user to discover.

### D9. Overdispersion is tested, and the correction is applied

σ_z = 1.463 — the week-to-week variation is 46% wider than binomial sampling can
explain (χ² = 60.0 on 13 df, p = 5.2×10⁻⁸). The Laney p′ correction is therefore
warranted.

Consequence, reported rather than buried: the naive chart flags **1** week
(W31); the corrected chart flags **0**. Widening the limits by 1.46× absorbs
every point.

The correct reading is *not* "the process is fine". It is that the weekly failure
rate varies far more than sampling explains, so a proportion chart at this grain
cannot separate signal from process noise, and the variation has to be attributed
another way.

**If wrong:** applying Laney when the data is genuinely binomial makes the chart
insensitive. Guarded by testing dispersion explicitly instead of assuming it, in
both directions (`test_sigma_z_is_about_one_for_binomial_data`).

### D10. Two centre lines are exported, deliberately

- `ucl` / `lcl` — centred on all 14 weeks (p̄ = 0.0664). Answers the
  retrospective question *"was this process ever in control?"*
- `ucl_phase1` / `lcl_phase1` — centred on Phase I (p̄ = 0.0307). Answers the
  monitoring question *"is this week consistent with baseline?"*

Same data, two different questions. The Power BI measures reproduce the
Phase I-centred version; agreement was verified to 8×10⁻¹⁷.

Within `p_chart`, the centre line and the dispersion estimate are separately
controllable. The centre must be pinned to a stable window; the dispersion
should use every subgroup available, because it is a variance component, the
moving-range estimator is insensitive to level shifts by construction, and
Phase I here is only five subgroups — far too thin to estimate a dispersion
factor from.

### D11. A g-chart was added, and its limitation is stated

To cover D8's blind spot, a runs-between-failures chart with exact geometric
limits. It needs no subgrouping and its upper limit responds to a single long
clean run.

**Stated limitation:** P(gap = 0) equals p exactly, so once p exceeds α/2 —
about 0.14% — the lower quantile pins to zero and no single gap, however short,
can breach it. At the 3.07% baseline this chart detects **improvement only**.
Asserted in `test_lower_limit_is_inactive_at_realistic_failure_rates` so it
cannot be quietly forgotten, and the result object emits a
`LOWER LIMIT INACTIVE` note at runtime.

This is why the g-chart is not the answer on its own.

### D12. Bernoulli CUSUM for degradation, threshold set by simulation

Shewhart charts judge each subgroup alone, so they react to spikes and are worst
at exactly what happened here — a sustained level shift. The CUSUM accumulates
the per-run log-likelihood ratio and needs no subgrouping at all.

Design: p₀ = 0.0307 (Phase I) → p₁ = 0.0614 (a doubling, as the smallest shift
worth detecting). Threshold h = 1.822 obtained by **simulation** to give an
in-control ARL of ~500 runs (achieved 509), not guessed. Simulation rather than
a Markov approximation because the Bernoulli CUSUM lives on a lattice of two
increment sizes, where continuous ARL approximations are poor at low p₀.

### D13. The CUSUM resets on signal

Found by inspecting the figure, not by reasoning: without a reset the opening
excursion drives the statistic to ~28, and since each passing run pays down only
log((1−p₁)/(1−p₀)) ≈ 0.031, it would take roughly 900 consecutive good runs to
fall back below the limit. The chart read as alarming for the entire 89 days,
including the five weeks the process was demonstrably healthy.

Reset-on-signal is how a CUSUM is actually operated: it signals, an engineer
investigates, the chart restarts against the process as it now stands. Covered
by `test_reset_prevents_a_permanent_alarm`.

## Inference

### D14. FDR control plus an effect size, always

590 sensors means 590 simultaneous tests; at α = 0.05 roughly 30 false positives
are expected under a global null. Benjamini-Hochberg q-values throughout
(validated against `statsmodels` to 2×10⁻¹⁶).

But significance alone is not enough in the other direction either: with n = 449
vs 717, **244 of 412 sensors (59%) shift significantly between regimes.** Only 8
have |Cliff's δ| > 0.5. A q-value here measures sample size as much as it
measures effect.

Rankings use the rank-based test and Cliff's δ rather than Welch and Cohen's d.
Several SECOM sensors have excess kurtosis above 60; for a number of them
Cohen's d reads ~0.15 while Cliff's δ reads ~0.65, meaning the distributions
separate cleanly but a mean-based measure is swamped by the tails.

### D15. A root-cause claim must survive the time confound

**71% of retained sensors show significant monotone drift against run order.**
The failure rate also changes over time. So any sensor that drifts will correlate
with failure for reasons that have nothing to do with causing it.

The gate applied: a sensor must discriminate fail from pass *within* the stable
Phase I window, where the regime is constant.

Effect on the candidate list:

| Screen | Sensors passing |
|---|---|
| Shift with yield regime (q<0.05) | 244 |
| Associate with failure, all runs (q<0.05) | 17 |
| **Also discriminate within stable regime** | **1** |

16 of the 17 whole-dataset hits have substantial drift and a non-significant
within-regime p-value. They are confounded, and reporting them as root causes
would be wrong. Only **S060** survives.

**If wrong:** without this gate the report would have named 17 root causes, 16
of them artefacts.

### D16. Forward chronological evaluation is primary; shuffled is a contrast

The published SECOM baselines use 10-fold CV, which trains on October to predict
August. Given 71% drift and a failure rate that halves partway through, a
shuffled split lets a model learn *when* a run happened.

A monitoring model can only see the past, so the forward split is the only
protocol that answers the deployment question. The shuffled result is computed
anyway, to measure how much optimism the shuffle buys.

Robustness: the forward result is not one lucky cut. Rolling-origin evaluation
across 8 cut points gives mean AUC 0.641 (sd 0.106). A grouped-by-week CV lands
at 0.652, between the two — showing the leakage is partly within-week and partly
future-information.

### D17. Class imbalance handled by weighting, not resampling

`class_weight='balanced'` rather than SMOTE or over/under-sampling, so no
synthetic rows enter the evaluation. With 104 positives, synthetic minority
points interpolated between neighbours would be difficult to distinguish from
leakage in a time-ordered setting.

### D18. Two model families, deliberately different

Regularised logistic regression is the reference — linear, inspectable, and the
closest match to the linear classifiers behind the published baselines.
Gradient boosting is there to test whether the ceiling is set by the model or by
the data. It is not better (forward AUC 0.565 vs 0.606), so the limit is
informational.

## Delivery

### D19. `fact_measurement` carries 35 watchlist sensors, not all 412

A full unpivot is 645,604 rows. Power BI handles that, but it refreshes slowly
and nobody scrolls 412 sensors. The watchlist — every sensor with tier-1 or
tier-2 evidence plus the top regime movers — gives 54,845 rows and matches how a
sensor chart is actually used. The full cleaned table is exported separately
(`data/processed/secom_clean_wide.csv`) for anyone who wants to widen it.

`dim_sensor` deliberately keeps **all 590**, including the 178 excluded ones, so
"why is this sensor not on the chart?" is answerable inside the report.

### D20. Where each calculation lives

| Calculation | Where | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Control limits | **DAX** | Must respond to slicers, or a chart filtered to one shift shows limits from all shifts |
| Centre line / baseline | **DAX, with `REMOVEFILTERS`** | Must *not* respond to slicers, or an excursion redefines normal as you drill into it |
| Robust z, Phase I limits per sensor | **Python, precomputed** | Depend on Phase I median/MAD; recomputing under filter context would silently move the baseline |
| CUSUM alarm *episodes* | **Python, precomputed flag** | Needs a row-ordered lag, which DAX does awkwardly and slowly |
| Triage capture rate | **DAX** | Deliberately interactive — the reviewer drags the budget slider and sees the weak lift themselves |

### D21. Timestamps exported as ISO-8601, all Power Query types declared explicitly

The raw SECOM timestamps are day-first (`dd/MM/yyyy`). Power BI applies file
locale on import and will silently read 07/10/2008 as 10 July. Separately,
`model_score` is null outside the forward test window, and Power Query's
type inference samples the first 200 rows, sees only nulls, and types the column
as text — breaking every downstream aggregation.

## Scope boundaries

### D22. No capability analysis (Cp / Cpk / Ppk)

Capability requires specification limits. SECOM publishes none, and the sensors
are unnamed and unitless, so no engineering limit can be inferred. Computing
capability against control limits instead of spec limits is a common error that
produces an authoritative-looking number with no meaning. Omitted deliberately.

### D23. No physical interpretation of any sensor

The signals are anonymised. S060 is described by its statistical behaviour only.
Naming it as a specific process parameter would be invention.

### D24. What this analysis cannot establish

- **Causation.** S060 is an association that survives a time-confound check.
  Confirming it needs a designed experiment, not more of this data.
- **Whether the W35 improvement was a process change or a measurement change.**
  A change in test limits or gauge would look identical in this data.
- **Generalisation beyond 2008 and beyond this line.** 89 days, one product.
- **S060's stability.** Its association is strong in W29–34 (δ = 0.21) and
  W35–39 (δ = 0.57) but **absent in W40–42** (δ = −0.04, p = 0.76). Either the
  mechanism changed or the association was never causal. Stated in the report as
  a caveat on the recommendation, not omitted.
